# Legal and regulatory considerations

Many choices turn on rules: what a legal form can do, whether procurement law applies, whether a profession needs a licence in a given place, whether subsidy or competition rules bite. The skill's role is to **identify** where rules could change the answer, **organise** what is known, and **draft the questions** for qualified advisers. It does not give legal advice; say so wherever conclusions appear ("This does not constitute legal advice; consult a qualified adviser").

## 1. Spot the categories that apply

Prompt the user with the categories relevant to the type of choice; examples are illustrative and jurisdiction-specific, so confirm which regimes apply:

| Category | Typical questions | Examples of rules (UK/EU-flavoured; adapt) |
|---|---|---|
| Subsidy / state aid | Does public support give an advantage? Market investor test? | Subsidy control regime; WTO subsidies agreement |
| Procurement | Must contracts be competed? Which route (framework, new framework, existing contract, exemption)? | Public procurement regulations; WTO Government Procurement Agreement |
| Funding / spending powers | Under what power is money spent? Whose budget? | Supply and appropriation rules; need for new spending powers |
| Contracting | Are binding commitments needed? Whose law governs? Contingent liabilities? | Contract law of client and supplier jurisdictions |
| Competition | Could the entity hold a dominant position and abuse it? Market sharing? | National and regional competition law |
| Aid / development funding | Tied-aid restrictions; development purpose test | ODA rules |
| Organisational form | What can each legal form do? Who can it contract with? What scrutiny applies? | Companies law; public bodies classification guidance; charity law |
| Professional regulation / licensing | Who may practise, under what title, in which place? | State or national licensing boards; interstate compacts |
| Data and IP | Who owns what; how data can be shared | Data protection law; IP licensing |
| Employment | Transfer of staff, pensions, secondments | Employment and pensions law |

Questions that apply to almost any new vehicle: What does it need to be able to do to function? How do existing guidance and legislation apply to it? How will it be funded, and under what power? How are relationships between multiple bodies governed (contract, MOU, shared services)? What risks and liabilities does each party assume?

## 2. Separate drivers from consequences

Some legal facts **drive** the choice (a form that cannot legally contract overseas is out). Others are **consequences** of the choice (the chosen model will need a procurement route). Put drivers into criteria or knock-outs; put consequences into the implementation plan and risks.

## 3. Commission advice in phases

A pattern that works:
1. **Reuse** existing advice, noting what it was written for and who may rely on it (advice given to one body often can't be relied on by another).
2. **Clarify** with the advisers in a short round of questions; capture the clarified view in writing.
3. **Commission new advice** split by question and by who commissions it (e.g. regulation and pricing for one party; competition and subsidy for another; a plain-English briefing note for wider stakeholders once both are done).

## 4. Comparing places (regulatory landscape)

When the type of choice is **Where**, regulation often differs by place. Present it as:
1. **Key definitions first** (e.g. what distinguishes a licensed profession from an unregulated one; licensure vs certification).
2. **Requirement × place matrix**: rows = requirements (licence needed for role A, B; title protection; registration board; membership of a mutual-recognition scheme), columns = places; ✓ / ✗ with short text where nuance matters.
3. **A tiered map or list** where many places apply: categories defined in words in the legend (e.g. "no restriction" / "restriction on reimbursement only" / "licence required, alternatives exist" / "licence required, no alternative").
4. **The implication** for the choice (which places are favourable and why), and the disclaimer.

## 5. Procurement routes (when the choice involves contracting suppliers)

Compare routes in a table with *approach / overview / pros / cons*: award under an existing framework; set up a new framework; use an existing contract; demonstrate an exemption (case-by-case legal review). Note that procurement rules typically require award on objective grounds and may prohibit excluding suppliers by nationality or appearing to pre-select suppliers.
