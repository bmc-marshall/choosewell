# Type of choice: What ("What should we do, and in what order?")

> **Supporting, not core.** Choosewell uses activity analysis mainly as an input to other choices (scope, structure, who). It isn't built principally for standalone activity prioritisation. If that's the user's main question, tell them so before starting (wording in SKILL.md, "Where Choosewell is lighter"), offer a structured first pass, and label the output as a starting point. Specialist operating-model or service-design work goes further: full process mapping, capacity and cost modelling, and workforce planning.

Use when a new or changing unit needs to decide which activities or functions to take on, which to leave to others, and how to phase them; or when a team needs to prioritise a list of initiatives.

## Default method

| Situation | Recommend |
|---|---|
| A new function in a landscape where others already do related work | **Responsibility mapping and diagnosis** (`methods/responsibility-mapping.md`) → **impact × availability** (`methods/matrix.md`) → scenarios → roadmap |
| A list of initiatives to prioritise, landscape not the issue | **Impact × availability** alone |
| Mandated work, deadlines, compliance | **Eisenhower** (urgency × importance), alone or as a check on the above |

## Steps (full version; skip what's settled)

1. **Frame** the function's aims and the value chain it operates in.
2. **Map** activities (verb + object) by value-chain stage; map organisations; plot as-is A / AD / D / S.
3. **Diagnose** each activity with the six issue types; draft the to-be.
4. **Narrow**: exclude internal set-up tasks, out-of-scope items, and support-only roles, each with a rule; show the waterfall.
5. **Score**: impact from evidence (counts of mentions, strength in key forums, inclusion in research), availability from difficulty, dependency, capacity, capability; place on the grid (3×3 default).
6. **Group into scenarios** (cumulative) and recommend a phased approach; state implications for existing organisations.
7. **Check spread and sensitivity** (does any axis fail to separate items?).

## Criteria starters

Impact: stakeholder demand, strategic importance, revenue or outcome potential, evidence in research. Availability: difficulty, dependency, capacity, capability, time to value. Eisenhower: urgency (deadline, legal requirement, commitment made, closing window), importance (contribution to agreed objectives).

## Outputs and storyline

Methodology (two phases) → value chain → responsibility heatmap with callouts → issue types → narrowing waterfall → focus areas highlighted on the value chain → scoring logic and grid → plotted matrix with key priorities → scenarios and roadmap → implications for existing organisations.

## Worked example (illustrative: a composite with details changed)

A new unit mapped the work in its field across six stages, plus back office and policy, starting from a standard process framework and refined in workshops with partner bodies. The as-is map showed accountability sitting mostly with central departments and most delivery in one existing body. The diagnosis led to actions for each activity: signpost, add people, redistribute, confirm ownership, create a remit, or stop. Mapping added nearly as many activities as the team started with; after excluding internal set-up, out-of-scope and support-only items, about 70 remained, roughly half new. Impact came from counting how often stakeholders raised each activity; availability from four factors the team scored. A dozen or so activities landed in the top-priority zone. Availability barely separated the activities, which was a signal to revisit that axis.
