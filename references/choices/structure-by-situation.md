# Type of choice: Structure by situation ("Which model fits *this* situation?")

Use when there is no single best answer in general: the right contracting, transaction, delivery or funding model depends on conditions specific to each opportunity, which emerge over time. Typical: how to structure a deal with a foreign government, how to contract a consortium, which delivery route suits a project, which funding method suits a client.

## Default method

**Decision tree** (`methods/decision-tree.md`): questions ordered by when information becomes available, red lines that force a branch, then push/pull factors where no red line decides. Plus fact sheets per model, cross-cutting risks, and the legal/procurement module.

## Steps

1. **Define the scope and assumptions** (e.g. "we are the supplier government; we are the signatory; the process covers identification to award"). State what is out of scope (e.g. the operating model of the vehicle itself, which is `choices/structure.md`).
2. **Define and order the models** by a meaningful dimension (resource required, risk), with an out-of-scope or non-commercial option for completeness, and set principles that apply to all (e.g. payment upfront in home currency where possible; fees follow public-money guidance; transparent communication with industry; a do-nothing option always exists).
3. **Fact sheet per model** with consistent fields (relationships at signature, staff, set-up time, risk, arrangements, liability, suppliers, procurement, revenue, risks in bid/in life, clearance, lessons).
4. **Distinguishing questions**, ordered by when they can be answered; mark what is known by each milestone (e.g. by expression of interest).
5. **Red lines** from both sides; options that are red lines for your organisation are discarded at the start.
6. **Push/pull factors** for the remaining choice.
7. **Cross-cutting risks** (opportunity cost; commercial; reputational abroad; reputational at home) and **legal/procurement** considerations (which procurement route; whether you can act as agent; regimes of the other party).
8. **Governance**: proportionate gateways linked to the model chosen; templates per gateway.
9. **Transitions and triggers**: models often change over an opportunity's life (e.g. supplier pending → supplier stated as partners are identified). Set out expected transitions as an adaptive pathway with triggers and lead times (`steps/09-decide.md` §9.2). Where rough probabilities and payoffs exist, quantify the tree (§7).

## Outputs and storyline

Definition and scope → three-layer framing → assumptions and principles → where the model decision sits on the opportunity timeline → model comparison table → the tree (and staged versions) → push/pull table → fact sheets → cross-cutting risks → legal and procurement routes → governance pathway → open questions → recommendations by time horizon (short / medium / long term) → asks of the board.

## Worked example (illustrative: a composite with details changed)

A government team that agrees commercial deals with other governments defined four in-scope deal models, ordered by how much of its own resource each needs, plus an out-of-scope non-commercial agreement. Three questions separated the models: are industry partners needed? Are they known when the deal is signed? Will the team itself contract with them? The client usually answers the first two early on. The third depends on red lines: the client insists on contracting with government, or wants to run procurement itself, or the team lacks the time to procure fairly. Where no red line decided, the team weighed what pushed it towards a model (resource during the deal, governance burden, control over reputation, fee income) against what the client was pulling for (simple procurement, low risk, deep partnership, speed). One model had a variant to meet client payment rules, with a legal question flagged. Each model linked to a proportionate approval route.
