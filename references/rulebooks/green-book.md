# The HM Treasury Green Book method

The Green Book is HM Treasury's guidance on appraisal and evaluation in UK central government. Its structure (rationale → objectives → long list → short list → preferred option, judged against critical success factors) works well outside the UK and outside government; drop or swap the UK-specific items flagged below.

**Currency check.** This summary reflects the **2026 edition** (published early 2026 following the Green Book Review 2025). Guidance changes: at the start of any UK public-sector appraisal, check gov.uk for the current edition and supplementary guidance, and tell the user if anything below has moved on.

## 1. The six steps

| Step | What it involves |
|---|---|
| 1. Rationale for intervention | Case for change; theory of change (how activities lead to outcomes); business as usual (not "do nothing"); evidence base |
| 2. Objectives | Up to ~5–6 SMART objectives framed as outcomes, not outputs |
| 3. Long list | Generate options via the options framework filter, informed by stakeholders, lessons learned, comparators |
| 4. Filter to short list | Assess against CSFs; RAG; record rationale; build the short list (§6) |
| 5. Analyse short list | Social cost-benefit analysis (CBA) and/or cost-effectiveness analysis; risk, optimism bias, sensitivity, distributional effects |
| 6. Select preferred option | Balanced value-for-money judgement; appraisal summary table |

Proportionality applies throughout: effort scales with the size, risk and novelty of the decision. Long-list work usually sits in a Strategic Outline Case; short-list analysis in an Outline Business Case; the Full Business Case revisits it after procurement. Ask which stage the user is at.

## 2. The five case model

| Case | Key question |
|---|---|
| Strategic | Is there a compelling case for change? |
| Economic | Which option gives the best social value compared with BAU? (Options appraisal sits here) |
| Commercial | Can a realistic, credible commercial deal be struck? |
| Financial | Is it affordable within public budgets? |
| Management | Can it be delivered: plans, governance, risk, benefits realisation? |

The cases are developed together and iteratively. Special features (legal, environmental) are handled as objectives, CSFs or costs and benefits, not as extra cases.

## 3. The options framework filter (long list)

Options are built from choices across five dimensions. Rate each choice Green (best meets CSFs) / Amber (meets them, less well; carry forward) / Red (fails; drop), usually in facilitated workshops with the rationale recorded.

| Dimension | Choices about… |
|---|---|
| Scope | What is covered (coverage, geography, services) |
| Solution | How it will be done (technical, process, service design) |
| Delivery | Who delivers (in-house, existing body, new vehicle, partner) |
| Implementation | When and how (phasing, big bang vs incremental) |
| Funding | How it is paid for |

Many "operating model" appraisals are really an appraisal of the **delivery** dimension; "activity" appraisals cover scope and solution; "transaction" appraisals cover implementation and funding. Saying which dimension you're appraising keeps the long list coherent.

## 4. Critical success factors and sub-questions

The 2026 edition names five CSFs. Below each are sub-questions drawn from practice; pick and adapt, and turn them into indicators (`methods/rag-success-factors.md`).

**1. Objectives and strategic fit** (meets the SMART objectives; fits wider strategies, programmes and policies). Treat this as a **gate**: an option that doesn't meet the objectives cannot be value for money, whatever else is true.
- Does it align with the stated ambition of senior stakeholders?
- Is it consistent with the organisation's values and ethics?
- Does it have (or can it obtain) the powers and remit needed?
- How broad and flexible a remit does it allow? How agile can it be (e.g. in taking on risk)?
- How robust is it in the long term (future-proofed, insulated from shifting priorities)?

**2. Value for money** (optimises social value: costs, benefits, risks)
- Does it allow demonstrable net benefits at system level?
- Likely operating costs; ability to generate revenue; treatment of surplus/profit and income to partners
- Can it operate at the scale required (many, large, long projects)?

**3. Supplier capacity and capability** (the supply side can deliver; payment and risk-transfer terms are acceptable)
- Is there a supply side able and willing to deliver (including, if procuring, available suppliers)?
- Clear lines of accountability; fits governance frameworks; works with existing organisations
- Supplier/partner confidence: reputational risk, likelihood of buy-in

**4. Affordability** (affordable for all public bodies involved)
- Budget to set up (infrastructure, legal personality) and to maintain
- Funding flows and methods for raising funds; cost of capital

**5. Achievability** (robust arrangements to deliver, monitor and evaluate)
- How complex is set-up in practice; can it be fast-tracked?
- Time lags (senior recruitment sign-off, legislation, stakeholder alignment)
- Access to the skills and commercial expertise needed; ongoing operation

## 5. Local criteria

The Green Book allows constraints such as legal requirements, ethical standards, social acceptability, timing and environmental policy to be treated as criteria. Add local criteria only where the context genuinely demands them, for example:

- **Regulatory landscape**: legislation and regulation, management of assets (e.g. IP), data security and information governance; powers the option could legally exercise; scrutiny required; legislative burden.
- **External confidence**: how the public, international buyers or partners would perceive it; reputational risk to the organisation or country.
- **Fit with the needs of a specific system** (e.g. "fit with the needs of the wider service it supports").

For UK public bodies, the 2026 edition requires statutory duties (e.g. the public sector equality duty, the biodiversity duty, the environmental principles policy statement) to be reflected at both long-list and short-list stages; add them as constraints or criteria.

## 5a. Weighted scoring (MCDA): when it's allowed and how

The Green Book supplementary guidance *Use of Multi-Criteria Decision Analysis in options appraisal of economic cases* (2024) sets firm rules:

- **Long list only.** Use MCDA at long-list stage, and only where the options framework (RAG against CSFs) hasn't produced a clear preferred way forward, typically complex trade-offs over scope and solution where no option dominates. **Not at the short list**: there, cost-benefit or cost-effectiveness analysis is required.
- **Simple scoring and weighting is not compliant** ("lack of transparency and objectivity"). Proper MCDA needs:
  1. Criteria underpinning the CSFs (at least one per CSF), validated with experts and stakeholders.
  2. Performance of each option on each criterion, scored in a workshop or written round; check for obvious errors.
  3. Scores consolidated in a facilitated decision conference; unresolved disagreements kept for sensitivity testing; options worst on every criterion dropped by dominance.
  4. **Swing weighting** (not importance weighting) and a ranking, sense-checked with the group.
  5. Sensitivity analysis on scores and weights: does the top option stay top?
- **Scoring**: for each criterion, best option = 100, worst = 0, others placed between (linear in the light-touch version; value functions where preferences aren't linear, e.g. diminishing returns).
- **Criteria must be mutually independent**; keep cost out of the criteria and weights; results are on an interval scale (no "twice as good").
- **Proportionality and expertise**: MCDA is resource-intensive; the guidance expects facilitators with operational-research MCDA competence and quality assurance in line with the Aqua Book (the government's analytical QA guidance: clear commissioner, analyst and assurer roles, proportionate QA, uncertainty communicated). A fuller approach is expected with 10+ criteria, criteria hierarchies, or non-linear preferences.

**What choosewell does for UK public-sector users:** recommends RAG against CSFs at long list (`methods/rag-success-factors.md`); offers swing-weighted MCDA as the next step where RAG leaves no clear preferred way forward, noting the facilitation and QA expectations; warns clearly if the user wants simple summed scores for a business case, and labels such output as not Green Book compliant.

## 6. Short list composition

Around five options:
- **BAU**: always carried forward as the benchmark, even if it misses objectives.
- **Do minimum**: just meets the objectives (exposes gold-plating).
- **Preferred way forward**: best combination of choices from the filter.
- **More ambitious** preferred way forward: more benefit, more cost or risk.
- **Less ambitious** preferred way forward: fewer benefits, lower cost or risk (can be dropped if it duplicates do minimum).

Other options may be added if they meet the objectives and CSFs. A group may also cap the short list for feasibility; if so, say that some ambers were excluded only because of the cap.

## 7. Short-list appraisal (economic case essentials)

Only where the depth requires it; often the user will commission this separately.
- Monetise where possible; otherwise quantify; otherwise describe qualitatively. Don't drop impacts that can't be priced.
- Adjust for optimism bias (raise costs and durations, lower benefits), shrinking the adjustment as risks are mitigated.
- Give central estimates with ranges; sensitivity analysis and switching values at least for the preferred option.
- Report net present social value, benefit-cost ratio and related metrics, but the BCR is **not** a decision rule: no arbitrary BCR thresholds; value for money is a balanced judgement including unmonetised impacts, risk, distribution and affordability.
- Present results in an **appraisal summary table** covering the full range of impacts per option.
- Where uncertainty is significant and the decision is costly to reverse, consider **real options analysis** (value of flexibility, decision trees, adaptive pathways); the climate supplementary guidance sets this out. How to do it: `methods/quantified-tree.md` §8 and `steps/09-decide.md` §9.1–9.2.
- Consider distributional and (where relevant) place-based effects; fraud risk where money is paid as grants or loans.

## 8. What changed vs older appraisals

If the user brings an older appraisal (e.g. pre-2022), update these points:
- CSF names (now "objectives and strategic fit", "value for money", "affordability", "achievability", without "potential").
- Strategic fit as a gate; theory of change in the rationale.
- Short list: BAU + do minimum + preferred + more ambitious + less ambitious (older appraisals often had "one viable alternative").
- BCR explicitly not a decision rule; appraisal summary tables.
- Statutory duties at both list stages.
- Recording the long-list rationale.

## 9. Using it outside UK central government

Keep: the six steps, the options framework filter, CSFs (renamed if helpful: strategic fit, value, delivery partners, affordability, achievability), the short list composition and the BAU benchmark. Swap: UK statutory duties → the legal duties that apply to the decision-maker; Managing Public Money and spending powers → the local public-finance rules; the discount rate → the local equivalent, if a CBA is done at all.

- [quick] Current Green Book edition checked (2026 at time of writing)
- [quick] Business case stage identified (SOC / OBC / FBC)
- [standard] Options framework filter: scope, solution, delivery, implementation, funding choices rated
- [quick] Five CSFs (+ justified local criteria); strategic fit applied as a gate
- [standard] Statutory duties reflected at long and short list (equality, biodiversity, environmental principles)
- [quick] No simple scoring-and-weighting in the business case; MCDA only at long list, swing-weighted, facilitated, if RAG gives no clear preferred way forward
- [standard] Short list: BAU, do minimum, preferred, more ambitious, less ambitious
- [full] Short-list CBA/CEA: optimism bias, ranges, switching values, appraisal summary table; BCR not a decision rule
