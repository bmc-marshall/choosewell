# Writing the outputs: clear, human, and open about how we worked

The people reading an appraisal are busy, smart and often new to the method. They need to understand what was done, why, and what it means for the decision, without decoding jargon or shorthand. Read this before writing anything the user will see: intake messages, method notes, workbooks, write-ups, storylines and slide content.

## 1. Sound like a thoughtful analyst, not a template

Write the way an experienced consultant would explain the work to a colleague: in full sentences, plainly, with a point of view.

- **Prefer sentences to fragments.** "We used swing weighting, which bases each weight on how much the gap between the best and worst option matters to you" beats "**Swing weighting** (recommended): *Why:* …".
- **Avoid the tell-tale AI patterns**: labelled mini-fields ("Why:", "Implication:", "Alternative:") on every point; bold scattered through sentences; strings of arrows (→) instead of verbs; rhetorical set-ups ("Here's the thing"); stacked hedges; lists of three adjectives; ending every section with a question. Use these devices only where they genuinely help scanning (a table, a short checklist).
- **Lead with the point**, then the reasoning. One idea per paragraph.
- **Use the reader's words** for their situation; introduce technical terms only when they earn their place, and define them when first used.
- **Be direct about judgement.** "On balance we'd choose B, because…" is better than "B could potentially be considered."

## 2. Explain every method the first time it appears

For each method or step, say in plain language **what it is, why we're using it here, and what it means for the result**, before using its name as shorthand. Example:

> We screened the suppliers in three stages. First, we removed any that failed a basic requirement, such as having no food-safety certification. These "knock-outs" aren't scored, because no score could make up for them. Second, we scored the remaining suppliers from 0 to 3 on four criteria, using written definitions of what each score means so that different people would score the same evidence the same way. Third, we grouped the results into tiers (front-runners, worth exploring, and so on) rather than relying on small differences in the totals.

Never leave a phrase like "knock-outs, then 0–3 scores with written definitions, then tiers" to stand on its own.

## 3. Show calculations, don't just name them

Whenever numbers are combined, show the arithmetic once in words and with a worked example, then use the result.

> We asked which improvement would matter most if each supplier went from worst to best on one criterion. Coverage mattered most, so it got 100 points; capability got 80, likely interest 50 and alignment 40. To turn these into weights that add up to 1, we divided each by the total (100 + 80 + 50 + 40 = 270). That gives coverage 100 ÷ 270 = 0.37 (37%), capability 0.30, interest 0.19 and alignment 0.15. A supplier's overall score is then each criterion score multiplied by its weight, added up: for example, 3 × 0.37 + 3 × 0.30 + 1 × 0.19 + 3 × 0.15 = 2.63.

Words like "normalise", "roll up", "switching value" or "regret" must be explained in plain terms the first time.

## 4. Label everything unambiguously

- **Criteria**: name each one in full and say what it asks ("Coverage: how many of our six regions they can serve"). Don't rely on abbreviations or codes (C1, C2) in anything a person reads; codes belong in the workbook only, next to the full name.
- **Score columns**: say they are scores and give the scale in the header ("Score 3 (best)", "Score 2", "Score 1", "Score 0 (worst)"; or "Coverage score (0–3)").
- **Weights**: show as percentages with the criterion name ("Coverage 37%").
- **Ratings**: always with a word or symbol, never colour alone; include a key.
- **Units and dates**: currency, time period, "as of".

## 5. Say how we worked: name frameworks and research

Readers should be able to see where each method or rule comes from, so they can judge it and look it up. Whenever an output uses a recognised framework, published guidance or research finding, name it briefly in the text and list it in a **"How we worked"** section (in the write-up and the method slide or appendix), using `presenting/sources-of-methods.md`. For each, say in one sentence what it is and how it shaped the work. Also say which choices were **our own judgement or defaults** (e.g. thresholds calibrated on a previous working group), so they aren't mistaken for external standards.

Example line: "Scoring independently before discussing comes from 'decision hygiene' research by Kahneman, Sibony and Sunstein (*Noise*, 2021), which found that group discussion before individual judgement amplifies the views of whoever speaks first."

## 6. Make the method notes conversational

At intake and when choosing methods, explain the choices in a short paragraph each, then invite questions once at the end:

> I'd suggest weighting the criteria using a method called swing weighting. Rather than asking how important each criterion sounds, it asks how much you'd value moving a supplier from the worst to the best on that criterion, given the actual suppliers in front of us. It's recommended in UK government guidance on multi-criteria analysis because it stops a criterion that sounds important but barely varies between options from dominating. If you'd prefer something simpler, equal weights work well for a quick screen. Happy to go into any of these choices in more detail.

## 7. Check before sending

Read the output as someone who hasn't seen the conversation: Would they understand every method and number? Is every score column labelled? Is every framework named with where it comes from? Does it sound like a person who knows the subject?
