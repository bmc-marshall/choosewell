# Step 9: Decide and explain

Make the choice, and explain it so that someone who wasn't in the room can follow and challenge it. Be honest about what could go wrong.

| | |
|---|---|
| Skip if | Never |
| You'll end up with | A recommendation with its reasons, what it keeps open, risks, conditions and next steps |
| Next | Step 10 |

## 9.1 Keep options open where it's worth it

When the future is uncertain, the choice is hard to reverse, and you'll learn over time, an option that keeps choices open can beat one that looks better on paper. This is option value (real options), which the Green Book's supplementary guidance on accounting for climate change recommends in exactly these conditions.
- Kinds of flexibility: **defer**, **stage**, **pilot**, **start small and expand**, **switch later**, **exit cheaply**.
- For the preferred option, say what it **keeps open** and what it **rules out**, and what flexibility costs (a premium, a slower start).
- Where numbers exist, compare flexible and inflexible versions with a quantified decision tree (`methods/quantified-tree.md` §8): the difference in expected value is the value of the flexibility. The same section covers which risky step to try first, and learning from one choice before making the next.

## 9.2 Routes with triggers (adaptive pathways)

Rather than one fixed choice, plan a sequence: start with option X; switch to Y when a **trigger** is reached; allow a **lead time**; name who watches the **signposts**. For example: host the unit in an existing body, and move to a company when external income passes £Z. Show it as a table of step, trigger, lead time and owner.

## 9.3 What could go wrong

- **Adverse consequences** (Kepner-Tregoe): for the top two or three options, list what could go wrong, rated for likelihood and severity (high / medium / low), with mitigations.
- **Pre-mortem** (Gary Klein): ask the group to imagine it's two years on and the choice has failed, and write down why. Feed the answers into risks, mitigations and next steps.

## 9.4 The recommendation

State:
- the preferred option and the main reasons it beats **each** alternative (or family of alternatives);
- what it keeps open and rules out;
- the risks that remain despite choosing it, and their mitigations;
- what would change the answer (switching values, Step 8.2);
- triggers for revisiting it, if adaptive;
- next steps, including who answers the remaining open questions.

If two options win on different criteria, recommend both with the reason for each so the decision-maker chooses the priority.

## 9.5 Decision quality check

Rate each element high, medium or low, and name the weakest; the decision is only as strong as its weakest element (Spetzler, Winter and Meyer, *Decision Quality*). Judge the decision by how it was made with what was knowable, not only by how it turns out.

| Element | Ask |
|---|---|
| Frame | Right problem, right scope? |
| Alternatives | Genuinely different options, including business as usual and flexible versions? |
| Information | Reliable and sourced? |
| Values | Criteria and weights agreed before scoring? |
| Reasoning | Numbers right; robustness tested? |
| Commitment | Decision-makers engaged and ready to act? |

## Checklist
- [standard] What the preferred option keeps open and rules out stated; flexible versions considered
- [standard] Adverse consequences rated for the top two or three options
- [standard] Pre-mortem run on the preferred option
- [quick] Recommendation gives reasons against each alternative, risks that remain, and what would change the answer
- [standard] Triggers set if the recommendation is staged or adaptive
- [standard] Decision quality rated and the weakest element named
