# Step 4: Choose how to assess (Tree 3)

Pick the assessment method that fits the options and evidence. This looks only at the options and evidence, plus the defensibility setting from Step 2; that's why it comes after the long list.

| | |
|---|---|
| Skip if | The method is prescribed (e.g. a Green Book business case uses red/amber/green against success factors) |
| You'll end up with | A method and a scale, explained in a short paragraph (workbook Frame tab) |
| Next | Step 5 |

## 4.1 Tree 3

**First, what kind of answer is needed?** Every appraisal is one of three:

| Kind of answer | Method |
|---|---|
| **Match**: a rule that picks the right option for each situation as it arises | `methods/decision-tree.md` |
| **Sequence**: many activities to put in order | If it's unclear who does what today, `methods/responsibility-mapping.md` first; then `methods/matrix.md` (impact and availability for what you *can* do; Eisenhower for what you *must* do) |
| **Select**: pick one, or shortlist a few, from a set | The table below |

**For selection:**

| How many options? | Then ask | Method |
|---|---|---|
| Many (more than about 12) | Hard data for the key tests? **Yes** | `methods/threshold-filter.md`, then assess the survivors with a method below |
| Many | **No**, mainly judgement | Knock-outs, then a light scored screen (3–4 criteria): `methods/scored-criteria.md` |
| Few | Must it be defensible (Step 2.3)? **Yes** | `methods/rag-success-factors.md` |
| Few | **No**, and it's one design choice with 2–4 options | `methods/pros-and-cons.md` |
| Few | **No**, otherwise | `methods/scored-criteria.md` |

Few means up to about eight; between eight and twelve, either route works (a light knock-out screen, then score). Knock-outs that rest on hard data (a certification, a licence) are applied in every route, whatever the number of options.

**If the group has already used a reasonable method**, keep it and test it (Step 8) rather than starting again; mention the default only if switching would change the result or the scrutiny it will face.

**Then, for any kind of answer: does the result hinge on a few uncertain events you can put rough numbers on, or does the choice come in stages (pilot, phase, wait, test first)?** If all the consequences can be put in money (or one common unit), the quantified tree **replaces** the method above and Step 5 is skipped. Otherwise add a **quantified decision tree** (`methods/quantified-tree.md`), usually on the two or three options left after the method above. It shows the expected value and risk of each option, what to do at each later decision, what information is worth, and the value of keeping options open. If the uncertainty sits in many continuous inputs instead, use a cash-flow model with ranges (same file, §11). If nobody can put probabilities on the futures, use scenarios and regret (Step 8.4).

Methods combine (a threshold filter on 40 places, then scored criteria on the five survivors, then a quantified tree on the last two). If costs and benefits can be put into numbers for the short list, add a costed comparison at the end (`rulebooks/` for the rules). Check the rulebook: UK central government business cases can't use simple points-based scoring (`rulebooks/green-book.md` §5a).

## 4.2 Choosing a scale

One scale per method; always show the word, number or symbol as well as any colour.

| Scale | Works best for | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| Red / amber / green (with split ratings) | Pass-or-fail tests; public-sector audiences | Define amber, or it becomes a catch-all for "don't know" |
| Scores 0–3 (or 1–3) | Ranking many options on a few criteria | Small differences in totals look more precise than they are; state a tie-break |
| One to three stars | Five to eight options compared visually | Say how the stars were used to choose |
| Four levels (undesirable / suboptimal / desirable / optimal) | Segments or markets on a few dimensions | Needs written definitions like any scale |
| ✓ / – / ✗ / ? | Building a rating from short statements | Needs agreed combining rules (`methods/rag-success-factors.md`) |
| 0–100, worst to best, per criterion | Formal weighted analysis, including Green Book long lists | Differences mean something, ratios don't: 80 isn't twice as good as 40 |

## 4.3 Explain the choice

Write a short paragraph: the method in plain language, why it suits this decision, what it means for the result, where it comes from, and the main alternative (SKILL.md, "Explaining your choices"; `presenting/writing.md`).
